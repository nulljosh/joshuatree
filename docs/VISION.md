## The north star

A computer that knows what you want before you ask. You open it at nine and your mail is already sorted. You finish a note and the reminder is already drafted. One tap takes it. Ignoring it costs nothing. It never nags, and none of it leaves your machine.

Not built yet. The first slice: Samantha learns your habits on the device (time of day, which app follows which, your calendar) and offers the next thing as a quiet suggestion.

## What Aspen, 1983 points at

At the 1983 Aspen design conference, Steve Jobs laid out where personal computers were going. Some of it maps onto this project. Some of it only rhymes.

**A motor in every appliance.** The electric motor started as one big machine, then drove a whole factory through belts, then shrank until every home had dozens. He said computers were going the same way, and the personal computer was that small motor. We push one step further than he did: the Pi kit is a whole computer you own and can read, not a share of someone else's.

**Past the "I Love Lucy stage".** He said new media copy old media first, the way early TV was radio with a camera. The new thing is that it answers back. Samantha should be the part that answers back, not a menu read aloud.

**You can't ask Aristotle a question.** Books let you read a thinker directly, but you can't ask them anything. He hoped a machine might one day capture someone's way of seeing the world so the next one could be asked. That is the closest anchor Samantha has. She is far smaller than that: an assistant on your desk, not a captured mind.

**A computer in a book, and a free sample.** His goal was a great computer in a book for under a thousand dollars. He also said people buy records because radio lets them hear them free first, and software has nothing like that. Our echo: a kit for less than a dinner, an OS that stays free, and a landing page that boots the real thing before you buy anything.

## How we work

Steve Jobs said great artists steal, after Picasso. So we take the best ideas openly, the Mac's menu bar, the Finder, the dock, and make them ours.

Every new thing gets seduced, then attacked, then ignored by the people it threatens. We expect all three. We seduce with the work itself (boot it live, talk to her). We name the enemy plainly: your life on someone else's computer. Then we ignore the incumbents and never compare ourselves to them.

A little anarchy: free, open source, no accounts, no tracking, runs on a small board anyone can buy. Held together by professionalism: every pixel finished, every claim true, every change tested before it ships.

Say no to almost everything. Make the few things that are left wonderful.
